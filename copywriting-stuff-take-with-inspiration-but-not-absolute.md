Converting Copy

Write copy that converts. Thinking comes before words. Interview first. Never draft from assumptions when the brief is thin.

This skill encodes a research-then-write system (six Ps of copy thinking, five Cs of internet writing, hook/body/CTA patterns, website page maps). Use it for sites, landing pages, product pages, about pages, pricing, emails, and ads — not only sales pages.

Hard rules





Interview before you write. If you lack a filled brief (People, Positioning, Promise, Proof, Priority, Process), ask questions. Do not invent an avatar, proof, or offer.



One page, one avatar, one promise, one CTA. Extra CTAs dilute action.



Copy is a window, not stained glass. The reader should see the offer, not admire the prose.



Pain of staying put beats vague pleasure for priority — stay honest. No fake scarcity, no invented testimonials, no medical or get-rich claims you cannot source.



Match the reader's language. Steal their words from reviews, sales calls, support tickets, and the interview. Do not sound like a brochure.



After drafting, run the quality checklist at the bottom. Fix failures before handing copy over.

Workflow

Interview → Six-P brief → Page map → Draft (hook → body → CTA) → Five-C edit → Checklist

Step 1 — Interview

Read references/interview.md. Ask in small batches (4–6 questions). Do not dump 30 questions.

Stop interviewing only when you can fill every cell of the six-P brief without guessing. If the user says "just write something," still extract: who it is for, what they get, why us, what proof exists, what they should do next.

Save the brief using the template in assets/brief-template.md.

Step 2 — Think in six Ps

Read references/six-ps.md before drafting. Fill:







P



Question





People



Who is this for, in their current messy reality?





Positioning



Why this brand over every obvious alternative?





Promise



What do they become / get? Outcome, not features.





Proof



Why believe you? Evidence a mute person could point at.





Priority



Why now? Cost of inaction, real deadline, or real limit.





Process



How does it work, and why other paths fail?

Do not write a headline until the promise passes the so-what test and proof is specific.

Step 3 — Map the page

Read references/website-pages.md and pick the pattern for the job (homepage, landing, feature, pricing, about, blog CTA, nav, error, etc.).

Default homepage spine:





Hook (who + outcome + credibility cue)



Problem / cost of the status quo



Promise



How it works (process, 3–5 steps)



Proof



Differentiation (positioning)



Objections



CTA (who, what, when, how)



Residual risk-reversal (guarantee, preview, no-pitch consult — only if true)

Step 4 — Draft

Read references/hooks-bodies-ctas.md.





Hook formula: benefit × relevance × credibility / perceived effort



Educational body: what → why → how



Story body: but / therefore (never then / then / then)



CTA: who + what + when + how

Write like a smart friend texting a busy person. Grade 5 reading level unless the avatar is a specialist (then use their jargon, still short).

Step 5 — Edit with the five Cs

Read references/five-cs.md.





Clear — firefighter test. Could someone shout this during an emergency?



Concise — every word costs $10. Cut without losing meaning.



Concrete — numbers, scenes, named objects. No "we help businesses grow."



Conversational — read aloud. If you would not say it, rewrite it.



Cadence — mix short, medium, long sentences. Squint at the shape.

Step 6 — Deliver

Return:





The six-P brief (short)



The copy, labeled by section, ready to paste



Optional variants (2–3 hooks or CTAs) only if useful



What you still need from the user (missing proof, legal claims, brand voice notes)

If implementing on a site, place copy in the actual files. Do not only dump text in chat when the user is building a page.

Quality checklist

Reject the draft if any box fails:





Avatar is named in language they use for themselves



Promise is an outcome, not a feature list



At least one proof item is concrete (number, name, artifact, timespan)



Process is easy to repeat in one breath



Single primary CTA



No sentence a firefighter would refuse to yell



No claim without a source the user provided



Reads aloud without stumble



Specific over generic ("14 leads to 70 leads" beats "more leads")



One idea per section

What not to do





Do not open with "In today's fast-paced world" or "Unlock your potential."



Do not use corporate sludge: leverage, synergy, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, utilize, empower, revolutionize.



Do not stack three CTAs ("Book a call, follow us, download, subscribe").



Do not write poetry. Do not write a school essay.



Do not fake urgency ("only 3 spots" when untrue).



Do not pitch the writing. Pitch the result.



Interview script

Ask in batches. Wait for answers. Quote the user later — their phrasing is the copy.

If they already pasted a site, deck, or offer, read it first and only ask what is missing.

Batch 1 — Job and people





What page or asset is this for (homepage, landing page, pricing, about, feature, email, ad, nav, other)?



What should a first-time visitor do when they finish reading (one action)?



Who is this for? Give a real person if you can (role, life stage, company size, what they were doing yesterday).



Who is this explicitly not for?



What does a good week look like for them after they use you? What does a bad year look like if they stay put?

Batch 2 — Promise and positioning





In one sentence, what do they get? Not what you deliver — what changes in their life or business.



What have they already tried? Why did those options disappoint?



Why should they pick you instead of the obvious alternative (do it themselves, hire an agency, buy the cheaper tool, ignore the problem)?



What do most people in your market believe that you think is wrong? What do you believe instead?



Run the so-what test with me: feature → so what → so what → until we hit a feeling or status they actually want.

Batch 3 — Proof and process





What proof can I point at without talking? Numbers, named customers (only with permission), logos, years, before/after, press, demos, time-to-result.



Tell me 2–3 customer sentences they have actually said (reviews, calls, emails). Paste raw if you can.



How does the thing work, in 3–5 steps a stranger could repeat?



How long until they see a first useful result?



What do you need from them (time, data, budget, skills)?

Batch 4 — Voice, constraints, priority





Words you always use? Words you never use?



Tone (pick two): warm, sharp, dry, playful, expert, plain, bold, calm.



Any legal, compliance, or claim limits?



Is there a real reason to act now (launch date, cohort, price change, seasonal cost)? If not, we will use cost of inaction — not fake scarcity.



Brand names, product names, and how they should be capitalized.



Pages or competitors whose voice you like — and what you like about them.

Empathy map (fill from answers, do not skip)





Current — what they do, see, hear, and feel on a normal day



Frustrations — the gravel in the shoe this week



Future fear (hell) — where they do not want to end up



Future goal (heaven) — the scene they want instead

Platinum rule: treat them the way they want to be treated, not the way you would want to be treated.

If they have customers already

Best research move: ask for sales-call notes, reviews, or support tickets. Repeat their beliefs and desires back to them. If you can describe the pain more clearly than they can, they assume you have the fix. The pain is the pitch — still stay kind and accurate.

Enough to write

You may draft when you have all of: avatar, outcome promise, at least one concrete proof item (or an honest "new, so here is process + risk reversal"), process in steps, and a single CTA. Flag every invented line in brackets like [NEED PROOF] rather than fabricating it.



Six Ps of copy thinking

The page is won before a sentence is written. Fill these in order.

1. People

You cannot answer "why us" until you know who "they" is.

Same offer, different avatar, different promise:





First-time marathon runner — fear of not finishing → "you will finish"



Busy professional — fear of falling off the plan → "a plan that fits a 60-hour week"



Runner over 40 — fear of injury → "finish and stay healthy"



Qualifier — fear of missing by seconds → "clear the mark with room"

Build an empathy map (current, frustrations, hell, heaven). Prefer one primary avatar per page. Secondary avatars get their own pages or a short branch, not a mashed headline.

Research hierarchy (best to worst): their exact words → reviews/tickets → interview with the user → your inference (label inference).

2. Positioning

How the offer sits in the reader's mind versus alternatives.

Positioning is often a fact you were too close to mention. Classic pattern (Schlitz / Claude Hopkins): every brewery filtered beer; only one said so first. "Everyone does this" is frequently the angle.

Ask:





What do we do that buyers assume is rare?



What do we refuse to do?



What category do we want to own in one phrase?

Strong positioning makes the brand easier to talk about. Other people should be able to repeat why you exist.

3. Promise

Who they become. Not the deliverable.

Jobs did not sell "5 GB." He sold "a thousand songs in your pocket."

So-what test (keep asking until you hit identity or feeling):





Artisan 5×5 silk rug → so what?



Large enough to anchor the room → so what?



Room feels pulled together → so what?



Guests feel the home is intentional → so what?



You feel like someone with taste and a home worth showing.

If the promise is still a feature ("AI-powered dashboard"), keep going.

Confused readers do not buy. One promise per page.

4. Proof

Trust is belief plus evidence. Belief without evidence is faith — fine for friends, weak for strangers.

Three kinds:





Authority by association — a named expert, publication, or institution (only if real)



Your results — specific arc with numbers and time



Customer stories — strongest. Match the avatar (a 50-year-old operator trusts another 50-year-old operator more than a campus case study)

Deaf-and-mute test. You cannot speak. You can only point.





Cannot point at "trusted by leaders" → point at logos



Cannot point at "proven results" → point at a number



Cannot point at "highly experienced" → point at "400 clients, 11 years"



Cannot point at "great copywriter" → point at "ads for a $106M launch in 3 days"

Proof is relative. Show people who look and sound like the reader.

No proof yet? Do not fake it. Lean on process clarity, a demo, a small guarantee you can honor, or work-sample upfront.

5. Priority

Why act now? People move toward pleasure or away from pain. They jog toward pleasure and sprint away from pain. Use pain of the status quo without cruelty.

Three honest levers:





Scarcity — real limited seats, inventory, or cohort size



Urgency — real end date



Cost of inaction — default and usually best for evergreen pages. What happens if they stay on the current path?

The gap between here and heaven creates deprivation. Deprivation is cousin to motivation. Name the gap in their words.

Never invent a countdown.

6. Process

How the offer works. Same outcome can have many processes (weight loss via training vs diet vs a clinician). The process you name is part of positioning.

A good process:





Explains why other solutions fail



Creates a believable path



Feels different



Is easy to understand

Formula: Everyone thinks X. That is wrong because Y. The solution is Z. Give Z a plain name if it helps memory. Do not invent fake trademark soup.

Example shape: "Everyone thinks more activity equals more progress. You scale faster by finding the one constraint and fixing that first."

Turning Ps into an ad or hero

Once the six cells are full, the first screen almost writes itself:





Name the person ("If your shop does $1–3M a year…")



Name the swamp they are in



Name the promise



Name the process in a phrase



Point at proof



Invite the one next step

Two tones, same skeleton: expert B2B can name the method; less initiated audiences often need the opportunity and proof of the person first, then the steps.



Five Cs — real-world writing

School writing rewards length, big words, and hiding "you." Internet writing is the opposite. Nobody is paid to finish your paragraph. Assume the reader is busy, distracted, and reading at a fifth-grade clip — unless they are a specialist, in which case use their terms and still keep sentences short.

You are not trying to sound smart. You are trying to make the reader feel clear.

1. Clear

They cannot say yes to a sentence they do not understand.

Edit by deletion. Start from the end: what must remain for the idea to survive?

Firefighter test. There is a fire downstairs. You run up.

Bad: "There may or may not be a situation downstairs and we should probably think about acting."

Good: "There is a fire downstairs. Open the window and jump."

Use a grader (Hemingway-style) when you can. Aim for short words, short sentences, one idea each.

2. Concise

No wasted words. In school, word count is a goal. Here every word costs $10.

Before you publish, ask: what can I cut without losing quality or understanding?

Practice constraint. A 280-character cap forces choice. If a line does not change what the reader believes or does, cut it.

3. Concrete

Make it visible in the head.

Bad: "We help business owners grow." Good: "We took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70."

When the idea is big and foggy (trust, quality, love of a craft), zoom in to a scene.

Foggy: "We care about the details." Scene: "We reprint the pack if the stitch line sits 2 mm off."

Not every line must be a movie. Meaningful claims should be picturable.

4. Conversational

Match the reader's in-group.

College students do not need "Please do not hesitate to reach out." They need "Text me if you need anything."

Lawyers do not need slang. Founders can take bluntness. Mid-market operators often want calm competence.

Read it out loud. If no human talks like that, rewrite. First person and second person are allowed. "You" is a feature.

5. Cadence

Writing should sound like music, not a stuck record.

Five-word sentences are fine. Several in a row drone. Vary short, medium, and long. After a rest, a longer sentence can carry weight.

Two tests:





Read aloud. Rewrite any stumble.



Squint at the paragraph shape. If every line is the same width, break it.

The mall-window rule

The best copy is the glass in a mall window. You should not notice the glass. You should see the thing in the store.

If the reader is noticing how clever the writing is, the writing is failing.



Hooks, bodies, CTAs

People read far more headlines than bodies. The hook spends most of the attention budget. Treat it that way.

Hook formula

Virality (or click-through) rises with:

benefit × relevance × credibility / perceived effort





Benefit — why they should care; what they get



Relevance — how many people this actually includes, and whether the reader sees themselves



Credibility — why you



Perceived effort — how hard it looks to read and to do. Lower the effort. "10 lessons" is easier than "a philosophy of work"

Example shape (thread hook):

"I was employee 30 at Facebook. Then I got fired. The lessons I learned from Mark helped me build a $100M-a-year company. 10 lessons from working under Mark Zuckerberg."





Credibility: employee 30



Benefit: 10 lessons



Relevance: lessons most builders can use



Effort: reading a thread instead of getting access to the person

Hook types that work on sites

Use one dominant type per hero.





Identity — "For independent shop owners who still answer every customer email"



Outcome — "Publish a site your sales team will actually send"



Problem — "Your homepage explains what you do. It does not explain why it matters."



Promise with terms — "We write the page with you in 14 days, or you do not pay the rest"



Cost of inaction — "Every week the old page stays up, you pay to explain yourself on sales calls"



Specific proof lead — "From 14 qualified leads a month to 70 — without adding a salesperson"

Do not write mystery hooks on a homepage. Curiosity works on social. Sites need recognition: "this is for me."

Body frameworks

What / why / how (teaching and feature pages)





What is the point



Why they should care (outcome, not trivia)



How they do it or how you do it for them

Example: Drink water before meals (what). Clearer thinking, better energy (why). 8 oz before each meal (how).

Use this to structure sections and even long pages: each H2 is a "what."

But / therefore (stories)

Linear "and then" stories are flat.





I went to the store, but I forgot my keys. Therefore I went home, but someone was in the kitchen.

Flip. Create a turn. Founder stories, origin blocks, and case studies should have a turn: belief → collision → new rule.

CTAs

You are not collecting applause. You are asking for a next step.

Who + what + when + how

"If you write your own site copy and you want a first draft in 20 minutes, download the brief template below and fill the six Ps."

Rules:





One CTA, one avatar, one offer per view



The button repeats the outcome, not the mechanics when you can ("See if we are a fit" beats "Submit")



Tell them what happens after the click (a 20-min form, a calendar, a sample chapter)



Secondary links (login, docs) live in the nav or footer, not in the hero

Value, if the copy includes an offer

Value is some mix of: save time, make money, reduce risk. If people stall, check:





Is it hard to say yes? Cut their time cost.



Is the money or time outcome fuzzy? Make it visible.



Do they doubt you can do it? Add proof or a smaller first step.

Speed, risk, ease. Move those three before you add adjectives.



Website page maps

Same six Ps, different jobs. Do not paste a sales-page onto a homepage or a homepage onto a pricing table.

Homepage

Job: the right people recognize themselves and take one next step. The rest leave without wasting a sales call.

Hero must answer in one screen: who it is for, what changes, why believe, what to do.

Then: problem, promise, process, proof, contrast with alternatives, objections, CTA.

Do not tell the company history above the fold unless the history is the product.

Landing page (single offer)

Job: convert already-warm traffic on one promise.

Tighter than a homepage. Remove nav that leaks. Repeat the ad's hook so the click feels consistent.

Long-form is fine when the price or commitment is high. Short-form is fine when the next step is cheap (email, demo booking).

Order that usually works: hook → problem agitation with their words → promise → proof → process → offer stack → objections → CTA → remnant proof.

Feature / product page

Job: connect a capability to an outcome for one job-to-be-done.

Each feature block: name the job, show the moment of use (concrete), state the outcome, add a tiny proof if you have it.

Avoid spec dumps in the hero. Specs can live lower for the buyer who needs them.

Pricing

Job: help them choose a plan without a meeting, or book the meeting if plans cannot.





Price each plan by outcome and limit, not by adjective ("Pro")



Say who each plan is for



Include the scary questions on the page (what happens if we cancel, who owns the data, how long to implement)



If you custom-price, say what the call is for and how long it takes

About

Job: transfer trust. Not a resume.

Lead with why the work exists and who it serves. Then the turn in the story (but / therefore). Then proof of character (how you work, what you refuse). Then a CTA that is the same as the site's primary CTA.

Team bios: one human line + one relevant credential. Cut the hobby paragraph unless it serves positioning.

Blog post / resource

Job: teach one point, then invite the next step.

What / why / how. CTA belongs after the point is earned, not before paragraph one, unless the page is clearly a gate.

Nav, footer, buttons, empty states, 404

These are copy.





Nav labels = words the customer uses ("Pricing" not "Investment")



Button = the outcome or the obvious verb plus object ("Book a 20-min demo")



Empty state = what to do next, not "Nothing here yet"



404 = one joke max, then a path home or to search

Multi-audience sites

Do not mash two avatars into one headline. Send them down a chooser ("I run a clinic" / "I build the product") or separate URLs.

Implementation notes for coding agents





Put the H1 on the actual problem/outcome, not the brand slogan



One H1 per page



Buttons and links must match the CTA you wrote



Keep line length readable; break hero subheads into two short lines rather than one wide paragraph



Alt text describes the proof image ("Dashboard showing 70 leads in June"), it does not stuff keywords
